The Curiosity Club. Episode 20. A Letter Written in Starlight.

Here is something to listen for. Is a light-year a measurement of time or distance?

Ready? Let us find out.

At an evening sky event, a guide points a telescope toward a star. Maya looks through it, then steps aside for Leo.

Are we seeing the star right now? she asks.

We are receiving its light now, the guide says. But the light left the star earlier. Light travels very fast, yet space is enormous.

Leo thinks of a postcard that arrives weeks after a vacation. The picture shows an earlier moment.

That is a helpful comparison, says the guide. The delay comes from travel time.

Pip asks whether stars need stamps. The guide tells him that light delivers the information without a postal service.

They discuss an imaginary star ten light-years away. Its light would take about ten years to reach Earth. A light-year is a distance, not a unit for someone's age.

Maya looks at Leo. So you cannot say you are twelve light-years old.

I can if I am a very confused birthday cake, he replies.

The guide explains that sunlight also takes time to reach us: about eight minutes. The club enjoys the evening sky without looking directly at the Sun.

On the way home, Maya imagines the sky as a collection of messages with different delivery times.

Pip writes his own message: Dear future listener, I hope you are still curious.

Leo adds: And I hope there are snacks when the message arrives.

Now, a little more to discover.

Light takes time to travel. A light-year is the distance light travels in one year. When we observe distant objects, the light shows them as they were when it began its journey. Sunlight takes roughly eight minutes to reach Earth, while light from other stars takes much longer.

Let us hear the main idea again.

The telescope collected light that had traveled through space. The club compared it to a delayed letter. They learned that a light-year measures distance and that looking farther into space also means seeing older light.
