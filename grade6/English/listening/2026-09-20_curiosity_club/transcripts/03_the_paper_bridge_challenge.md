The Curiosity Club. Episode 3. The Paper Bridge Challenge.

Here is something to listen for. What did Maya change in the first improved bridge?

Ready? Let us find out.

The science club has a challenge. Build a bridge between two books using one sheet of paper. Then see how many small blocks it can hold.

Leo lays his paper flat. This is a very fast bridge, he says.

The first block stays on it. The second block makes the middle bend. The third block falls through, taking the bridge with it.

Fast to build, Maya says, and fast to fall.

Pip offers to hold the paper up. Ms. Park smiles. The bridge has to work without a robot underneath it.

Maya folds her paper back and forth, like a small fan. She puts it across the same gap. It holds more blocks before it bends.

Did you use thicker paper? Leo asks.

No. We used the same kind. I changed the shape.

Leo wants to make the gap smaller, add tape, and use two sheets at once.

Let us change one thing first, says Maya. Then we can understand what helped.

They fold a new sheet and test it over the original gap. This time, they write down the result. They also put the blocks in the same place each time.

Pip names the bridge The Great Paper Highway.

Can a bus cross it? he asks.

A toy bus, perhaps, says Leo.

Pip makes a sign: Small vehicles only. Large robots must go around.

Now, a little more to discover.

An object's shape can affect how it bends under a load. Folding paper can make it harder to bend in some directions. Engineers compare designs, test them, and improve them. When you change one feature at a time, it is easier to understand why a result changed.

Let us hear the main idea again.

A flat paper bridge bent quickly. Maya folded the paper and tested it across the same gap. The new shape held more blocks in their test. The club learned to compare designs carefully and keep a record.
