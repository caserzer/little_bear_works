The Curiosity Club. Episode 23. The Shadow That Changed Its Mind.

Here is something to listen for. Did the pole need to move for its shadow to change?

Ready? Let us find out.

At nine in the morning, Maya marks the end of a pole's shadow with chalk. Leo writes the time beside the mark.

At lunchtime, they return. The shadow has moved and changed length.

The pole is walking when nobody watches, Pip suggests.

Maya checks its base. The pole has stayed in the same place.

They return once more in the afternoon and add another mark. Now their three observations tell a small story about the day.

Ms. Park explains that a shadow forms when something blocks light. The Sun's apparent position in the sky changes as Earth rotates. That changes the direction and length of the shadow.

In the classroom, they use a flashlight and a toy figure. When the light is low, the figure makes a long shadow. When the light is higher, the shadow becomes shorter.

Leo places a tiny hat on the figure. The shadow gets a hat, too.

Pip is delighted. A complete fashion show with only one model.

Maya notices that the experiment is useful but not a perfect copy of the Sun and Earth. Their flashlight is much closer than the Sun.

They place the day's marks on a drawing and label the times.

Could we use shadows to tell time? Leo asks.

People have done that with sundials, says Ms. Park.

Pip adds one condition: The clock may take a break on a cloudy day.

Now, a little more to discover.

A shadow appears where an object blocks light. Its size and direction depend on the arrangement of the light, object, and surface. Earth's rotation makes the Sun appear to move across the sky. Sundials use changing shadows to indicate time when there is suitable sunlight.

Let us hear the main idea again.

The pole stayed still, but its shadow changed during the day. A flashlight model showed how light direction affects a shadow. The club used time labels to turn separate observations into a useful record.

Remember our listening question? Did the pole need to move for its shadow to change?

Here is the answer. No. The direction of the sunlight changed as Earth rotated, so the shadow changed while the pole stayed still.
