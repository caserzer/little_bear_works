The club prepares for a map game. Maya has a compass, and Leo has a map. Pip has already marked the snack cupboard as the most important landmark.

Why does the needle keep pointing that way? he asks.

It responds to Earth's magnetic field, Maya reads from their guide. One end points roughly toward magnetic north.

Pip rotates the compass case toward the snacks. The needle turns relative to the case and settles again.

It is very stubborn, he says.

Ms. Park brings two classroom bar magnets. The children discover that some ends pull together while others push apart.

Opposite magnetic poles attract, she explains. Like poles repel.

Leo moves a magnet near the compass. The needle changes direction.

Now it is listening to me!

It is responding to the nearby magnet, Maya says. That is why we should keep magnetic objects away when using a compass for directions.

They also learn that magnetic north and geographic north are not exactly the same. For today's simple classroom game, they use their teacher's marked directions.

Pip puts the magnet aside and follows the map properly. He reaches the reading corner.

No snacks here, he reports.

But there is a book about explorers, says Leo.

Pip chooses it. A compass can help you find a direction, he concludes, but you still have to decide where you want to go.

Now, a little more to discover.

A compass contains a small magnet that aligns with the surrounding magnetic field. Nearby magnets and some metal objects can interfere with its direction. Magnetic north is not identical to the geographic North Pole. Opposite magnetic poles attract, while like poles repel.

Let us hear the main idea again.

Turning the compass case did not make the needle choose the snack cupboard. A nearby magnet did change its direction. The children learned both how a compass helps and why nearby objects can interfere.
