The classroom is divided into two pretend islands. Maya stands on one side with a small flashlight. Leo stands on the other with a notebook.

No shouting across the ocean, Ms. Park says. Your challenge is to send a message with light.

Maya flashes three times. Leo writes: Three snacks, please.

That is not what I meant, she says.

Pip writes: Three robots arriving.

Also wrong.

They realize they need an agreed code. They choose one short flash for yes and two short flashes for no. A longer pause separates messages.

Now Ms. Park asks questions. Is the library open? One flash. Is Pip a penguin? Two flashes.

Pip requests a third answer: Occasionally, during dance practice.

The children later try a simple letter chart. They discover that sending letters takes patience and that unclear pauses can change the message.

Ms. Park explains that people have used many kinds of signals to carry information, including light, sound, and electrical pulses. A code connects patterns with meanings.

Leo deliberately sends one unclear pattern. Maya asks him to repeat it instead of guessing.

That is good communication, Ms. Park says. Checking a message can be part of the system.

Pip sends two careful flashes and then holds up a sign: No penguins detected.

Leo looks relieved. The classroom ocean is safe for another day.

Now, a little more to discover.

A signal can carry information when sender and receiver share a rule for interpreting it. Codes assign meanings to patterns. Timing and separation can matter as much as the individual signals. Real communication systems also need ways to handle errors or ask for information to be sent again.

Let us hear the main idea again.

Random flashes produced different guesses. Once the children agreed on a code and pauses, they could exchange messages. When a pattern was unclear, asking for a repeat worked better than inventing an answer.
