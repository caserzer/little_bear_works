The Curiosity Club. Episode 28. The Ice Cube's Hidden Side.

Here is something to listen for. Why can ordinary ice float in liquid water?

Ready? Let us find out.

Leo drops an ice cube into a clear cup of water. Part of it sticks out, but most of it is below the surface.

It is a very small iceberg, Maya says.

Pip draws a tiny ship on paper and holds it beside the cup. The captain reports a much bigger object underwater.

Why does the ice float? Leo asks. It is solid, so I thought it would sink.

Ms. Park explains that being solid is not enough to decide whether something floats. Ordinary ice is less dense than liquid water. For the same volume, it has less mass.

They use the idea of two equal-sized boxes. If one contains more mass packed into that space, it has greater density. The boxes are only a comparison; water molecules are not little toys in boxes.

Maya notices that the cube keeps turning slowly. She draws the visible part above the line and the larger part below it.

A photograph showing only the top could be misleading, she says.

Leo thinks of seeing only one page of a mystery story. You might miss most of the evidence.

Pip gives the paper ship a new instruction: Respect what you cannot see.

As the ice melts, the club watches its shape change.

Our iceberg is getting smaller, says Maya.

Pip folds up the ship. The captain will now explore a much drier place: the bookshelf.

Now, a little more to discover.

Ordinary ice floats in liquid water because it is less dense. Water expands when it freezes under familiar everyday conditions. Most of a floating ice cube or iceberg stays below the surface. Density compares mass with volume; size alone does not tell you how dense something is.

Let us hear the main idea again.

Most of the ice cube was underwater. The club connected floating ice with lower density and learned that the visible top does not show the full size of an object.
