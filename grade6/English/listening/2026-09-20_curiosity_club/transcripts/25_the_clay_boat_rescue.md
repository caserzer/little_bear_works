The science club has a small tub of water and a lump of modeling clay. Leo rolls the clay into a ball and drops it in. It sinks.

Pip announces a rescue mission for the clay submarine.

Maya takes the clay out and dries it. What if we change its shape without adding or removing any clay?

She makes a wide little bowl with raised sides. When she places it carefully on the water, it floats.

The material is the same, Leo says. So why is the result different?

Ms. Park asks them to notice how much space the bowl shape takes up. Water pushes upward on objects. A floating boat displaces enough water for that upward push to balance its weight.

The hollow shape keeps water out while allowing the boat to push aside more water before it is submerged.

Leo puts a small block into the boat. It sits lower. He adds more, one at a time. Eventually, water comes over the edge and the boat sinks.

Pip writes a passenger rule: Do not invite the entire classroom.

They make a second boat with higher sides and compare the designs. They record their results instead of assuming that every wider or taller boat will always be better.

At the end, Pip thanks the rescue team.

The submarine has returned to boat life, he says. It has requested a smaller guest list.

Now, a little more to discover.

Water exerts an upward force called buoyancy. An object floating at rest displaces water whose weight matches its own. A boat's overall shape, including the space that keeps water out, matters. A material that sinks as a solid lump can be part of a floating hollow boat.

The same clay sank as a ball and floated as a hollow boat. Adding blocks made the boat sit lower until water came over the edge. The experiment connected shape, displaced water, and carrying capacity.
