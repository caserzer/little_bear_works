The club builds shapes with strips of cardboard joined at the corners. Leo makes a square. Maya makes a triangle.

Pip declares the square the winner because it has more corners.

Ms. Park asks them to push gently on the shapes. The square changes into a slanted shape as its corner joints turn. Maya's triangle keeps its shape more easily without bending the strips.

The number of corners was not a useful test, Pip admits.

Maya adds a diagonal strip across the square. Now it is divided into two triangles, and it resists the same gentle push better.

They look at pictures of bridge frameworks and roof supports. Triangles appear again and again.

Does that mean every bridge must be a triangle? Leo asks.

No, says Ms. Park. Engineers use many kinds of structures. The material, connections, shape, and expected loads all matter.

The club improves a cardboard tower for its exhibition. They add braces where the frame was leaning and test it with the same small load as before.

Pip designs a banner: Team Triangle. Excellent at standing still.

Leo asks whether he should make his sandwich triangular for extra strength.

That depends on your engineering goal, Maya replies.

My goal is to eat it.

Then cutting it into triangles may help your lunch, she says, but it will not prove anything about bridge safety.

Pip records another important design lesson: Always ask what problem you are trying to solve.

Now, a little more to discover.

A triangle made of fixed-length sides is geometrically rigid, while a four-sided frame with freely turning corners can change shape. A diagonal brace can divide such a frame into triangles. Real structural strength also depends on materials, connections, and loads; shape alone is not a complete safety test.
