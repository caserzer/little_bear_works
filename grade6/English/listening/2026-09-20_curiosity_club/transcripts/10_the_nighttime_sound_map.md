The Curiosity Club. Episode 10. The Nighttime Sound Map.

Here is something to listen for. What information returns to a bat after its call reaches an object?

Ready? Let us find out.

At sunset, a nature guide takes the club to a park. Everyone stays on the path and watches the sky.

A small shape flies above the trees. Then another appears.

Are those birds? Maya asks.

They are bats, says the guide. Bats are mammals, and they can truly fly.

Leo has heard that bats cannot see. The guide explains that bats have eyes and are not blind. Many also use sound to find their way and catch insects.

She turns on a special detector. It changes some bat calls into sounds people can hear. The speaker makes quick clicks.

Pip listens carefully. That sounds like someone typing a very urgent message.

A bat sends out calls, the guide says. Sound bounces off objects, and returning echoes give it information.

Like shouting in a large empty hall? Leo asks.

The idea of an echo is similar, though a bat uses much finer information.

Maya imagines a picture made from returning sounds. The bat turns quickly, then disappears over the pond.

Could I find my missing sock by shouting at it? Leo asks.

You could begin by looking under your bed, says Maya.

Pip records a message for the sock: Please report your location.

There is no reply. Some mysteries still need a flashlight and a careful search.

Now, a little more to discover.

Many bats use echolocation: they produce calls and listen to returning echoes. Those echoes can help them locate objects and prey. Many of the calls are too high-pitched for human ears. Bats also have vision, and different species rely on different senses to different degrees.
