The Curiosity Club. Episode 17. Who Took a Bite of the Moon?.

Here is something to listen for. Does the Moon actually lose a piece when it looks like a thin smile?

Ready? Let us find out.

Maya draws the Moon on Monday. It looks like a thin smile. Several evenings later, she draws it again. This time, the bright part looks larger.

Who is changing it? Leo asks. Perhaps someone keeps taking bites and then putting them back.

Pip searches his imaginary menu. Moon pie: one enormous serving.

Ms. Park brings a ball and a lamp into a darkened classroom. The lamp represents the Sun, and the ball represents the Moon. Maya stands in one place to be the observer on Earth.

The lamp lights half the ball. As Ms. Park moves the ball around Maya, Maya sees different amounts of the bright half.

The whole ball is still there, Maya says. I just cannot see all of its lit side from here.

Exactly, says Ms. Park. The Moon reflects sunlight. Its changing position gives us different views of its sunlit half.

Leo asks whether Earth's shadow makes the ordinary phases. Ms. Park explains that Earth's shadow causes a lunar eclipse when the objects line up appropriately. That is a different event.

Pip writes a notice: The Moon has not been eaten. Please cancel the emergency bakery meeting.

That evening, Maya looks up again. She draws both the bright shape she can see and a faint circle to remind herself of the whole Moon.

It is still round, she says. My view is what changes.

Now, a little more to discover.

The Moon does not make its own visible light like the Sun. We see sunlight reflected from its surface. As it orbits Earth, we see different portions of its illuminated half. These views are called phases. Ordinary Moon phases are not caused by Earth's shadow.

Let us hear the main idea again.

The ball stayed whole while Maya saw different amounts of its lit half. This helped explain the Moon's changing appearance. Earth's shadow belongs in the explanation of a lunar eclipse, not the ordinary monthly phases.

Remember our listening question? Does the Moon actually lose a piece when it looks like a thin smile?

Here is the answer. No. We are seeing only a small part of its sunlit half from our position on Earth.
